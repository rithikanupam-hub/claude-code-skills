# The story engine: the dopamine loop

From the founder (2026-09-30), described as the same psychology casinos use. Each step makes the viewer need the next one. The full source is Kallaway's Dopamine Ladder (six levels), table below.

## 1. Stakes (first 3 s): make them care
Stakes have three parts:
- **A character** someone can root for: a real person, a group, or "you".
- **Something at risk**: a job, a deal, a life, a language, being understood.
- **Urgency**: a deadline, a moment, "right now".
The stakes don't have to be life or death. They have to feel personal to our viewer: people who work, meet and build across languages.

## 2. The big question (by about 7 s): make them curious
Give just enough context that one juicy question forms in the viewer's head ("which card comes next?"). Stakes buy attention; the question keeps it. Hint at it, then name it out loud, or let it land unspoken. Never answer it before the head fake.

## 3. The head fake: make them hooked
Everything so far leads the viewer to assume **A**. Then the story turns and reveals **B**. That contrast is what makes a story addictive. The B must be true and sourced, found in research, never invented for effect. Good head fakes flip:
- a common belief ("translation is about words" → it's about ...),
- a hero or villain (the person we thought failed ...),
- a cause (we blamed X, the data says Y),
- a scale (it's not rare, it's most people).

## 4. Validation: close the loop (confirmed, Kallaway's "Dopamine Ladders", 2026-10-01)
"Validation comes when you do end up giving them the answer to the question that you popped in level two... closing the loop." For education, the reward is the real insight the viewer can use. Make it non-obvious, and deliver it clearly with a hard cut, a match cut or the resolution shown on screen. Then **reload**: open the next, bigger question as you close this one.

## The full Dopamine Ladder (source: notebook `q8.md`)
Our four steps sit inside Kallaway's six levels:
| Level | What happens | Our step | Where it is done |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Stimulation | 0–2 s: the eye reacts to contrast, motion, colour and brightness before meaning ("a visual stun gun") | the first frame | motion-director: a vivid first frame that moves |
| 2 Captivation | the viewer asks themselves a question (the curiosity loop) | stakes + big question | script |
| 3 Anticipation | the viewer guesses the answer; dopamine peaks just *before* the reveal; head fakes prolong it | build-up + head fake | script, plus held shots in the edit |
| 4 Validation | the answer, a non-obvious payoff | payoff | script, with a hard or match cut |
| 5 Affection | the viewer starts to like the messenger | value + a consistent voice | the same narrator voice, look and sign-off every film |
| 6 Revelation | "they'll keep giving me value": seeing our name triggers the hit | the series | a recognisable series format, published consistently |
Faceless content struggles at level 5. Our substitutes are real value (the strongest driver for education), one recognisable narrator voice, one recognisable look, and a recurring series name.

## Loops inside the loop
In a 60–75 s film, run one main loop and one or two mini loops (a small question, answered 10–15 s later with a small head fake). Rule: **open a new question before closing the current one.**

## Default shape (70 s)
| Time | Beat | Loop step |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 s | A strange, specific line with a character and something at risk | Stakes |
| 3–8 s | Context, then the question | Big question |
| 8–25 s | Build the viewer's assumption A with evidence, history, data | Setup (mini loop 1) |
| 25–40 s | "But..." Reveal B, with a source on screen | Head fake |
| 40–55 s | Why B is true: the insight, a philosopher or researcher, a number | Payoff (mini loop 2) |
| 55–66 s | What it means for the viewer's work and life | Payoff |
| 66–70 s | The bigger open question, then the end card | Reload |

## Example of the shape (facts must still be checked in research before use)
Stakes: "1945. Twenty-one men on trial, four languages, and nobody in the room could follow everything."
Big question: "How do you hold the trial of the century if nobody understands each other?"
Assumption A: they took turns, and one interpreter translated each sentence after it was spoken.
Head fake B: they did something that had hardly been tried: people translating **while** the speaker talked, through headphones. It was the birth of simultaneous interpretation.
Payoff: understanding is a human skill we keep reinventing tools for.
Reload: "So when machines can translate everything, what's left for people to do?"
