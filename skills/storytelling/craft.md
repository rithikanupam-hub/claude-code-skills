## 1. Find the core first: the 5-line blueprint (Tim Runia)
Before any beat table, write the story in five lines. If you can't, you don't have a story yet. "If you don't know what the story is really about, no editing trick or camera trick is going to save you."
1. **Situation:** where we are, what's happening, and who is in it.
2. **Desire:** what the character (or researcher, or viewer) wants. This is where people lean in.
3. **Conflict:** what gets in the way. No obstacle means there's no reason to keep watching.
4. **Change:** the turning point, which can be an insight, an action or a new way of seeing. This is where our **head fake** lives.
5. **Result:** the new reality: how the world, or the viewer's understanding, differs from the start.
For research stories: situation = the consensus or baseline; desire = the question; conflict = the contradiction or gap; change = the discovery; result = what it means now. The five lines are a launchpad. Expand them to 10 or 20 lines without changing the core.

## 2. Move it: context and conflict, "but / therefore"
- A story alternates **context** (closes a loop in the brain) and **conflict** (opens one). Never stay in context for long.
- Matt Stone and Trey Parker's rule: if beats are joined by "and then", the story is a list. Join them with **"but"** (a complication opens a loop) or **"therefore"** (a consequence moves things forward). Check every pair of beats.
- Short videos can carry up to four conflict loops in the first 30 s (Kallaway's Stanley Cup breakdown: "I'm consistently setting up these head fakes to keep the storyline moving").
- For research: "Researchers assumed X, **but** the data showed Y, **therefore** they tested Z, **but**..." Never "in 1954 this happened, and then...".

## 3. Rhythm: the jagged-edge test
Put every sentence on its own line and look at the right margin. If the line ends all fall at about the same point, it drones. A jagged margin is music: short, medium, and now and then one long sentence that builds. Gary Provost's "This sentence has five words..." passage is the model. Rhythm in the edit is the same: let a key shot breathe. Fast cuts are not always better.

## 4. Hooks: the 4 S's and the triple hook (Kallaway)
- The hook must answer, within milliseconds, **what is this about?** (the subject) and **what do I get if I keep watching?** (the stakes, or outcome).
- **The 4 S's:** a clear **Subject** (a person, object, place or mission); **Stakes** (for education, the understanding the viewer gains or the mistake they avoid); **Speed** (compress the words, don't talk faster); **Simplicity** (one meaning only, no clever wordplay; if the viewer has to think for half a second, they're gone).
- **Triple hook:** the spoken hook, the text hook and the visual hook must say the same thing. Someone watching with the sound off should be able to guess the spoken line. Misalignment is the most common failure. Show while you tell: the image lands before the words do.
- **Text hook rules:** subject word plus a teaser (a pain, an outcome or a change); radical specificity ("22 days", not "a few weeks"); at most two lines, broken at natural phrase pauses; no punctuation except quotation marks; no "and/but/that"; on screen for at least 3 s.
- **Don't:** "Wait till you see this", "You won't believe...", or any hook that hides the subject.

## 5. The lock-in zone (the 2–4 sentences after the hook, about 5–10 s)
This confirms the hook was not a bait-and-switch, and the viewer decides whether to trust us. For us, the trust anchor is **proof**: put the source on screen (the study, the archive photo, the data) inside the first 10 s. Add packaging quality (clean frame, clear audio) and relatability (the viewer's world: meetings, clients, colleagues).

## 6. Make information feel like a story
- **Specs → human experience:** "5 GB" is a spec; "1,000 songs in your pocket" is something people feel. Turn every number into what it means for a person.
- **A story lens:** don't just pick a topic. Pick the angle nobody else takes (for us: the human cost of not understanding each other).
- **Teaching vs connecting:** decide the one feeling or truth the viewer leaves with *before* writing.
- **Hyper-specific:** exact numbers, dates, names and physical details instead of generalities.
- **Talk to one friend**, not to an audience. Avoid a lecture tone.

## 7. Endings
- **Write the last line first** ("the last dab"): the line people would quote. In short videos it can loop back into the first line.
- The result ties the story together and shows how the viewer's understanding changed.
- A call to action that serves the viewer (what to notice, what to try), never "follow for more". Ours is the brand end card, nothing more.

## 8. Don'ts (sources' anti-patterns)
"And then" storytelling; vague or clickbait hooks; a monotonous rhythm; effects on every cut ("the viewer feels the edit"); talking *at* people; hooks whose spoken, text and visual layers don't match; rushing complex ideas.

## Our tension to manage
Ref5 cuts every 0.8–1.5 s, and the notebook warns against over-editing. Resolution: cut fast on **evidence lists** and conflict beats, and **hold** on the head fake, the key number and the last line (1.5–3 s, music dipped). Keep effects to one transition style per film.
